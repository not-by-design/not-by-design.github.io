# 6 — Learning Research Simulation Methods

Backlog: [6 — Build a personal learning program for research simulation methods](../CONTENT_BACKLOG.md#technology)

## Aim

Develop practical understanding of how research simulations are specified, explored, and evaluated. The priority is learning the methods and their limits, not producing a publishable result or building a polished game. Small programming exercises are a bonus path for making the methods concrete.

The starting distinction comes from the supplied note “Game simulation vs. research simulation”: a game simulation is commonly judged by the experience it creates, while a research simulation must make its assumptions and claims inspectable. A simulation derives consequences from assumptions; by itself, it does not establish that those assumptions describe the world.

## Guardrails

- Keep each opportunity small enough to complete independently and produce a visible artifact: a field map, comparison, model specification, runnable program, or short reflection.
- Separate illustration and existence proofs from calibrated or experimental evidence. Do not describe a toy model's output as a prediction or empirical finding.
- Make assumptions explicit, vary important parameters, and use repeated runs when randomness affects outcomes.
- Ground behavior rules and parameter choices in observations or a clearly stated mechanism. Abstraction is useful, but a familiar optimizing agent or borrowed parameter value is not self-justifying.
- Represent people as purposeful but fallible actors when the question calls for it: their knowledge, attention, reasoning, habits, social learning, and institutional setting may shape what they do.
- Start coding with the Python standard library so setup does not become the project. Add a framework only if it serves a clear learning goal.
- Treat public-facing presentation as a possible later application, not a requirement for this methods-learning program.

## Methodological concern: human action and model assumptions

The program should address the concern raised by the supplied note and its reference to “Masonomics”: a model can misdescribe human action if it treats people as frictionless optimizers with fixed preferences and complete command of relevant information. People act for reasons, but can misunderstand situations, hold conflicting aims, rely on habits or rules of thumb, learn from others, and deceive themselves. Which of these matters depends on the question and setting. Arnold Kling's [“Notes on Masonomics: Reason without Rationality”](https://www.econlib.org/archives/2009/04/notes_on_masono.html) is a useful entry point, alongside the wider methodological and behavioral literature.

This is not a rule against neoclassical models, mathematical abstraction, or simplifying assumptions. The test is whether each assumption fits the question, has a defensible basis, and survives comparison with plausible alternatives. A parameter copied from a standard model is a hypothesis to justify, not an observation about people.

For each candidate behavioral rule, record:

- **Claim about action:** What are actors trying to do, and what choices are actually available to them?
- **Knowledge and reasoning:** What do they know, misunderstand, attend to, or learn? Do they calculate, follow routines, imitate, or use rules of thumb?
- **Social and institutional setting:** Which norms, relationships, organizations, rules, and power differences shape their options and actions?
- **Evidence and scope:** Which observations support the rule or parameter—field or experimental data, historical records, interviews, administrative data, or prior findings—and to which population and context do they apply?
- **Alternatives:** What credible behavioral rule could replace it, and would the model's main result change under that alternative?
- **Limits:** Which motives or contextual features remain unmodeled, and what conclusions therefore exceed the model's reach?

Observed choices do not reveal motives on their own. Treat evidence as contextual and potentially ambiguous; use multiple sources where feasible, and distinguish a directly measured quantity from a calibrated or assumed parameter.

## Project opportunities

### 1. Survey the simulation field

**Question:** What kinds of questions are addressed by agent-based models, microsimulation, system dynamics, and macroeconomic models, and what does each approach make easier or harder to represent?

**Work:** Build a concise comparison matrix covering purpose, unit of analysis, behavioral assumptions, data needs, typical outputs, validation approach, and common failure modes. Read introductory material on model documentation, including ODD for agent-based models.

**Deliverable:** A two- to four-page field map with a short glossary and a list of questions that seem suitable for simulation.

### 2. Map candidate applications

**Question:** Which real-world questions are interesting enough to explore, yet narrow enough to model responsibly?

**Work:** Generate a small set of candidate applications. For each, state the mechanism of interest, what would be represented, what would be left out, what evidence could inform the assumptions, and which conclusions a model could and could not support. Compare at least one market or institutional question with a non-economic example to avoid letting one domain define the whole method.

**Deliverable:** A ranked shortlist of three candidate questions, each with a one-paragraph model sketch and an explicit claim ceiling.

### 3. Ground behavioral assumptions in observed action

**Question:** What evidence supports the model's account of how people act in this setting, and what credible alternative accounts should be considered?

**Work:** For one candidate application, describe the actors, available choices, information, constraints, social relationships, and institutions before selecting behavioral rules. Find a small set of relevant observations or prior findings. Build an assumption ledger that links each rule and parameter to its evidence or theoretical rationale, states its scope, and marks what remains conjectural. Compare a default optimizing rule, if relevant, with at least one evidence-motivated alternative such as bounded search, habit, rule-following, imitation, or social learning. Do not presume either version wins; identify what observation could discriminate between them.

**Deliverable:** An assumption ledger and a short evidence note that distinguish observations, interpretations, and modeling choices.

### 4. Specify a model before coding

**Question:** Can the model's agents, state, rules, environment, and time steps be described clearly enough that someone else could reproduce the design?

**Work:** Choose one small example, such as a threshold-based neighborhood sorting model or a simple resource-and-trade model. Write a compact specification, draw the update sequence, list assumptions, and identify which model outputs would be informative. Record what the model is intended to illustrate and what it cannot establish.

**Deliverable:** A one-page model specification and a short ODD-style description.

### 5. Reproduce a small model by hand

**Question:** How do local rules produce aggregate patterns, and how sensitive is the result to those rules?

**Work:** Trace several steps of the chosen model by hand using a tiny grid or population. Change one rule or threshold at a time and note how the outcome changes. Compare the observed pattern with the model's stated purpose rather than treating a visually striking result as proof.

**Deliverable:** A worked example with a state table or annotated diagram, plus notes on the mechanism and the limits of the demonstration.

### 6. Coding stretch: implement and explore a toy model

**Question:** What becomes easier to see when the model can run many times?

**Work:** Implement the selected toy model in plain Python. Keep model rules separate from display/output code. Make the random seed, population or grid size, and key behavioral threshold configurable. Run multiple seeds, summarize outcomes, and vary one parameter at a time. Save a small table or chart and explain what changed and what stayed uncertain.

**Deliverable:** A runnable, documented script or notebook, a brief model description, and a small results summary. This is an exercise in model behavior, not evidence about real populations.

### 7. Coding stretch: add a research workflow

**Question:** What checks help distinguish a robust pattern from an artifact of one setup?

**Work:** Add a repeatable experiment that compares several parameter settings across multiple random seeds. Report distributions or ranges rather than only one run. Check limiting cases, record the code and parameter choices, and identify at least one plausible alternative explanation for the result. Where the model permits it, compare behavior rules grounded in different evidence or mechanisms and see whether the main result depends on one choice. If the baseline exercise is comfortable, try implementing a second simple model or using a modeling library and compare the setup burden.

**Deliverable:** A reproducible experiment folder with model notes, run settings, summarized results, and a short sensitivity/robustness reflection.

### 8. Choose a next research question

**Question:** What would be required to move from an illustrative model toward a model that can inform a real-world claim?

**Work:** Revisit the candidate applications and identify data, calibration targets, validation evidence, domain expertise, and alternative explanations needed for the strongest candidate. Consider whether the right next step is more reading, a better model, or no simulation at all.

**Deliverable:** A one-page next-step memo with a precise research question, evidence requirements, major risks, and a go/no-go recommendation.

## Suggested sequence

Start with Projects 1 and 2, then ground and document assumptions in Project 3 before specifying the model in Project 4. Choose either the hand-worked or coded route in Projects 5–7. Project 8 closes the loop. The coding projects are optional; completing the field map, an assumption ledger, a model specification, and the next-step memo still constitutes a coherent methods-learning cycle.

## Completion criteria

- Explain the difference between illustrating a mechanism, showing that a mechanism can generate an outcome, fitting a model to data, and collecting behavioral evidence.
- Describe a small model's assumptions and update rules in a way another person could follow.
- Trace each behavioral rule and key parameter to observations, a stated theoretical rationale, or an explicit modeling choice, and describe the limits of that basis.
- Explain how a plausible alternative account of human action could affect the model's result.
- Explain how parameter changes and repeated runs affect the interpretation of its outputs.
- State what evidence would be needed before making a real-world claim from the model.
- If completing the coding stretch, run the model repeatedly and reproduce a summarized result from recorded settings.
